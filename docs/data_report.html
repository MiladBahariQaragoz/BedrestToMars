The shape of it

What we are holding

Three CSV files, one schema. Every row is a single baseline → follow-up contrast for one muscle, in one study arm, at one timepoint — never a raw measurement standing on its own.

61 columns, grouped by what they answer

The anatomy of one row

This is a real record, unedited — Tran 2021, the AGBRESA campaign, gluteus maximus at day 59 of −6° head-down tilt. The 61 columns look unreadable as a spreadsheet line, but they only answer seven questions. Read it once in this shape and the rest of the file becomes obvious.

How much of the schema is really filled in

The single most important thing to understand about this dataset: pct_change is complete for every row, but the absolute value_followup exists for only 30 %. Most papers publish the change, not both endpoints. So this is a percent-change dataset that happens to carry raw values where it can — not a table of measurements we can recompute from.

pct_change, unloading rows only, muscles with n ≥ 4

Which muscles actually waste

The headline pattern the talk rests on. Soleus leads the loss; psoas and the obturators gain volume during bed rest, which is a real result and the reason the colour scale here is signed rather than a single hue.

duration_days × phase

The duration axis, and the gap in the middle of it

Rows cluster at the campaign lengths the field actually runs: short 5–14 day studies, then a jump to the 60–90 day flagship campaigns. Between day 35 and day 60 there is almost nothing, and that gap is exactly where a Mars-transit extrapolation has to pass through.

study_id · cohort_id

Where the rows come from — and why it is fewer studies than it looks

51 papers, but only 35 independent cohorts. 10 campaigns were published more than once, and one campaign supplies half the dataset on its own. This is the whole argument for validating leave-one-cohort-out rather than leave-one-paper-out.

modality · outcome_type · design · data_source

How the numbers were measured

Overwhelmingly MRI volume read out of a printed table. That homogeneity is good for comparability and bad for coverage — a model trained here knows almost nothing about ultrasound thickness or DXA lean mass.

sex · age_mean · n_analysed · population

Who was actually in these beds

Young men, in groups of eight to twelve. Female rows are a small minority and older participants rarer still — a limitation to state out loud in the talk rather than discover in the Q&A.

Arms and countermeasures

A little under half the rows come from an arm that was doing something to resist the atrophy. Any model that ignores arm_type will read countermeasure success as a weaker effect of bed rest itself.

Everything we know is imperfect about it

Every judgement call made during extraction was written into the row rather than smoothed away. This section is the list of things that could bite us.

No row has been double-extracted by hand

double_extracted is FALSE for all 747 rows. An AI-assisted re-extraction of the 580 rows that existed on 4 September, checked against the original papers, agreed on 556 and left no numeric conflict unresolved; its corrections are applied and logged in data/reconciliation_log.md. It is not a human double extraction, so the flag stays FALSE.

14 rows are spaceflight, not bed rest

They carry exposure_flag = spaceflight and are kept in the file deliberately. Whether they join the training set or are held back as an external test is still open — the flag exists so that it stays a decision.

Nineteen included studies yielded no rows at all

Three are XML-only, where the text is available but the figure images are not; the rest plot changes with no baseline printed anywhere, so a percent change could only be built by inventing the denominator. Per-paper reasons are recorded in screen_decisions_fulltext.csv.
